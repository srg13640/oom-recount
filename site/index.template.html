Where Aschenbrenner’s forecast stands

In June 2024, Leopold Aschenbrenner, a former OpenAI researcher, published Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead, a 165-page argument that straight-line trends in computing power, algorithms, and spending would produce AI able to do an AI researcher’s job by 2027, and superintelligence by the end of the decade. Every “his” on this site refers to him. The projections are his; the observations are sourced. The essay is cited as S1.

Bottom line: The money and the are arriving as fast as Aschenbrenner said they would, or faster. AI that can work on its own for hours () showed up a year or two earlier than he expected, and on written tests the best AI passed PhD-level experts in 2024. Two things are slower than his plan: no single is yet close to the size he expected for 2026, and the US power grid is growing far more slowly than his path needs. His big claim, an AI that can do an AI researcher’s job () by 2027, is still open; at today’s rates it lands between mid-2027 and 2028.

How to read this site

One question runs through every page: two years on, were his numbers right? Each chart puts what he projected in 2024 next to what has actually been measured since.

  • Blue is his forecast: solid where it was already history, dashed where it was a guess, shaded where he gave a range.
  • Gold is what actually happened. On the charts, tap any gold point for the figure and its source; the S-numbers link to the source list. On the comparison rows below, tap a row to open the chart it comes from.

Counting the OOMs again

Bottom line: Aschenbrenner measures AI progress in : how much useful computing power went into the best model, counting both more chips and smarter methods, in steps of 10× (each step is an ). By that yardstick the best models are about 3.4 steps past GPT-4, roughly two-thirds of the way to the point where he expected an AI that could do an AI researcher’s job. On actual tests, AI passed PhD-level experts in 2024, and the pace of improvement nearly doubled once arrived. What has not been shown is the last rung: an AI doing a researcher’s job on its own.

His Figure 1, redrawn. Effective compute is physical training compute times algorithmic efficiency (better methods that get more out of the same chips), normalized so GPT-4 = 1. The observed points use Epoch AI’s physical-compute estimates plus assumed algorithmic gains since GPT-4 at the rate you set below; nobody can measure the algorithmic part directly.

His summary graphic: compute and algorithms move models along the OOM line; “unhobbling” (chat training, reasoning, tools, computer use) widens what they can do with it. The gold marker is the 2026 frontier at your settings.

Measured capability

The closest thing to a single intelligence score: Epoch AI combines results from more than 50 benchmarks onto one scale, fixed so Claude 3.5 Sonnet = 130 and GPT-5 = 150. The scale is arbitrary (not IQ, not a percentage); what matters is the slope. Epoch puts the frontier at about 14 points a year since reasoning models, versus about 8 before S2 S41.

The test he singled out as the hardest unsolved one in 2024, with Claude 3 Opus near 60% and in-domain PhDs near 80%. He expected it to fall within a generation or two. It fell within three months: o1-preview scored 78% in Sep 2024, and OpenAI said it beat the PhD experts it recruited S47. Scores above about 90% sit near the test’s ceiling, since some of its questions are themselves flawed S45.

Physical compute

FLOP counts the arithmetic operations used to train a model. Public estimates for closed models are thin after mid-2025 S7, so GPT-6 Astra carries a range of 5×10²⁶ to 2×10²⁷.

What this meansThe raw ingredients, computing power and better methods, are growing at about the rate he assumed, no faster. The surprise is how much capability each step bought once models learned to reason step by step: the test that was hardest in 2024 was passed within months. But a is a floor, not a ceiling. Scoring like a PhD on a quiz is not the same as doing a PhD’s job, which is why the Agents tab matters more for his 2027 claim.

Clusters, chips, and power

Bottom line: The giant he predicted for 2026, about one each (the output of a large power plant), are being built on schedule, and chip production is keeping pace. But those sites are shared across many jobs, so the biggest single on record used only about a quarter of one. And the official outlook for the US power grid in 2030 is a small fraction of what his path would require.

Epoch AI’s measured trend: about 7 months S8. Longer is likely if power stays the bottleneck.

Power

Not a perfect match: his figures cover AI alone and use US electricity as a yardstick (he noted not all of it would be in the US). The observed figures cover every US data center, AI or not, because that is what gets measured. Even so, official 2030 outlooks of about 9–17% are far below his 100%.

What this meansBuildings, chips, and money are not the bottleneck he worried about most; electricity is. Companies can announce 5-gigawatt campuses, but connecting them to the grid takes years, and the official forecasts have data centers at roughly a tenth of US electricity in 2030, not the whole of it. If his timeline slips, this is the most likely reason.

Spending and revenue

Bottom line: Spending on AI is running ahead of Aschenbrenner’s 2026 estimate and on course for his $1-trillion-a-year call for 2027. Revenue, the money actually coming in, reached his $100-billion milestone in mid-2026, though spread across two AI labs rather than at one tech giant as he expected.

Capex is capital spending on things like data centers and chips. Big-tech capex is a subset of his measure (it leaves out other clouds, labs, and governments) but includes some non-AI spending.

2025 to 2026: +77%. 2026 to 2027, analyst view: about +38%. He assumed total AI investment would roughly double each year.

Revenue run rates

A run rate is a recent month’s revenue times 12, not audited annual revenue, and the two companies may count differently. His milestone was a tech giant like Google or Microsoft earning $100B a year from AI; no tech giant reports that figure, but OpenAI (≈$40B) and Anthropic (≈$65B) passed it together by August 2026 S17.

What this meansThe spending is real and so is the revenue, which is the strongest sign this is not only speculation: the two leading labs now take in more than $100 billion a year between them. Two cautions. Capex guidance is a plan, not money spent, and run rates are not audited revenue. And the spending only continues if the revenue keeps growing.

From chatbot to agent

Bottom line: This is where reality ran fastest. Aschenbrenner expected that a little would let models keep working on a problem far longer without a person steering. It did, starting with OpenAI’s o1 in Sep 2024 S28. Within 19 months, the best model could finish tasks that take a skilled person 17 hours, about half the time.

What is measured: , an independent research group, gives AI models tasks (mostly software work), times how long skilled humans need for the same tasks, and reports the longest task a model completes half the time (the 50% ) or four times in five (80%).

Since 2024: about 3.5–4 months. 2019–2024: about 7 months S26.

Time horizon (METR) is the length of task, timed on skilled humans, that a model completes a given share of the time. It is not how long a model can run unattended, and 50% success is far from reliable; the 80% view is the better guide to dependable delegation S23.

His rungs come from his table converting a model’s “thinking time” into human working time. Laying them over METR’s measure is an analogy, not his metric.

What this meansTwo years ago the best AI could handle a few minutes of work before losing the thread; now it handles a couple of workdays, about half the time. “Half the time” is the catch: at 80% reliability the horizon is far shorter, which is why today’s agents are useful for drafts and code but are not yet trusted with week-long jobs. His 2027 claim needs the 50% line to reach weeks and the 80% line to catch up.

Method

His projections come from the June 2024 essay S1. Between rows of his tables (each dated mid-year), values are interpolated on a log scale; elsewhere his stated growth rates are extended. Charts are redrawn in the essay’s style: his estimates in blue (solid for the past, dashed for projections, shaded for his stated range), observations in gold.

“How far off his line” divides the observed figure by his projection for the same date. On track means within about 1.4× either way; bars show the observed range where sources give one.

Effective compute for observed models = Epoch’s physical estimate × 10 to the power of (algorithmic rate × years since GPT-4). The algorithmic rate is your slider; it cannot be measured for closed models.

Derived figures are marked as derived: Nvidia’s annualized sales (quarter × 4), 2024 big-tech capex (backed out from 2025’s reported +73%), 2026 chip shipments (from Epoch’s stock estimates), and the Nvidia comparison line (his mid-2024 figure grown at his own 2024–26 investment pace). Trend lines on the capability chart use Epoch\u2019s published rates (about 8 points a year before 2024, about 14 since reasoning models), drawn through the index\u2019s two calibration points.

Several sources are secondary summaries of Epoch AI or METR data, or of press reports; benchmark scores are company-reported. Run rates and capex guidance are company figures, not audited results. Treat single-source numbers (marked in each readout) as provisional.

The public pages make no network requests. Three series (Epoch’s capability index, Epoch’s training-compute estimates, METR’s time horizons) are pulled from the publishers’ own data files whenever the owner rebuilds the site; everything else is hand-entered.
